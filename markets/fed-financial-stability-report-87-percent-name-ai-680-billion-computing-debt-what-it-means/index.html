The Fed's Financial Stability Report: 87% of market contacts now name AI as the top risk, and $680 billion of computing debt is a third of investment-grade issuance. What the numbers mean before Nvidia and Anthropic report

AI has gone from a 30% mention a year ago to the single most-cited threat in the Fed's survey, and the report puts hard figures on the financing stack behind it: $1.1 trillion of outstanding public debt, up to $3 trillion of commitments not yet started, and close to $1 trillion of big-bank exposure.

Why it matters
FinanceThe Fed is now modelling the AI build-out as a late-1990s telecom cycle and says it may soon exceed it. The $3 trillion of debt, guarantees and leases on infrastructure not yet commenced is the number to put next to every hyperscaler and neocloud credit you hold.
ExecutivesThe survey closed before Huang resigned and before the midterms. If 87% of market contacts named AI as the salient risk then, the number this week is higher. Treat AI-linked counterparties, including your own cloud vendors, as a concentration you report to the board.
SecurityThe Fed lists state-actor cyberattacks and 'damage caused by frontier AI models themselves' alongside valuations and financing as the three AI risks to the system. That is a central bank saying model misbehaviour is a financial-stability vector.

The Federal Reserve's semi-annual Financial Stability Report, released on Friday November 13, is the first in which artificial intelligence tops the list of risks named by the market contacts the New York Fed surveys. 87% of them cited AI-related risk, against 50% in the May report and 30% a year earlier. The next most-cited concerns were a broad correction in risk assets at 52%, higher long-term rates at 48%, and cyberattacks and fiscal sustainability tied at 43%. The survey ran in September and October, which means it closed before Jensen Huang's resignation from Nvidia, before the Anthropic prospectus and before the midterm elections.

Three channels, with numbers attached

The report groups its AI concern into three channels. The first is valuations: equity prices remain high, in the Fed's words partly on enthusiasm for super intelligence, and earnings that fall short could trigger a correction. The second is cyber, where the report names sophisticated state actors and, in a phrase worth reading twice, damage caused by frontier AI models themselves. The third is financing, and this is where the report adds data that was not public in one place before. Debt issuance tied to advanced computing reached $680 billion by October 2026, five times the total for all of 2023, and now accounts for nearly a third of nonfinancial investment-grade corporate issuance, up from under 6% three years ago. Outstanding public debt tied to advanced computing exceeds $1.1 trillion. Debt, guarantees and leases on AI infrastructure that has not yet commenced could exceed $3 trillion. The largest US bank holding companies have close to $1 trillion of exposure to large technology firms, data-centre developers, computing-related special purpose entities, private credit vehicles and utilities. The Fed compares the cycle with the late-1990s broadband and telecom build-out and says it may soon surpass it; the links among data-centre firms, public credit, private lenders and SPVs, it writes, create vulnerabilities that require close monitoring.

Why the timing matters

The report lands at the start of a week in which the two most important AI issuers price. Nvidia reports its third quarter on Tuesday with interim CEO Colette Kress presenting; consensus is about $108.8 billion of revenue, and the company's $500 billion-plus of third-party financing commitments is exactly the kind of off-balance-sheet structure the Fed is describing. Anthropic prices its IPO on Wednesday with a reported order book several times covered; its prospectus discloses about $901 billion of computing commitments, about $70 billion of them due within twelve months. The Fed does not name either company, but a reader can map its $3 trillion figure onto the disclosed commitments of a handful of firms.

The counter-argument

The case for calm is in the same report. Household and business debt vulnerabilities are rated moderate and the banking system sound. Investment-grade issuance by cash-generative hyperscalers is not 1999 telecom debt issued by companies with no revenue, and the $680 billion is spread across issuers with some of the strongest balance sheets in the index. A survey of 23 market contacts is a sentiment gauge, not a stress test, and sentiment that is 87% one way is often already priced. The Fed's own concern is less that the debt defaults than that it is interconnected: a bank lending to a developer, holding a SPE's paper, financing a private credit fund that lends to the same developer, and serving the utility that powers the site.

What to watch

Nvidia's Tuesday call for any change to its financing programme and lease guarantees, which are now a regulator's talking point as well as a governance one. Anthropic's pricing on Wednesday and whether the $26 billion placement from its suppliers is read as demand or as circular financing. The next FSR in May, which will show whether the 87% was a peak. For treasurers, the practical step is an exposure map: which of your vendors, lenders and counterparties sit inside the Fed's $1 trillion of bank exposure, and what happens to your cloud pricing and credit lines if one of them has to refinance in a worse market.
