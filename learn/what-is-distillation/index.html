What is distillation? How small models learn from big ones, why Inkling 2 Mini used a trillion tokens of it, and why OpenAI's new usage policy bans it

Distillation trains a smaller, cheaper model to imitate a larger one. It is how most of the models you can run on a laptop got good, it is the technique behind the cheapest API tiers, and as of November 20, 2026 it is explicitly forbidden with OpenAI's outputs. Here is how it works, what it costs, and where the legal and security lines now sit.

Updated 2026-11-23 · For: Beginners Developers ML engineers Executives Security

Distillation is teaching by imitation. A large, expensive model, the teacher, answers a great many prompts. A smaller model, the student, is then trained to produce the same answers. The student never sees the teacher's weights or training data; it learns from the teacher's behaviour. Done well, the result is a model a tenth the size that keeps most of the teacher's ability on the tasks the prompts covered.

How it works

The original 2015 idea, from Geoffrey Hinton and colleagues, was to train the student on the teacher's full probability distribution over possible next tokens rather than only on the single correct answer. Those soft targets carry far more information per example: they tell the student not just that the answer is 'Paris' but that 'Lyon' was plausible and 'banana' was not. Modern practice at frontier scale is simpler and larger. The teacher generates millions of prompts and completions, often including its reasoning, the outputs are filtered for quality, and the student is trained on them as ordinary text. Thinking Machines' Inkling 2 Mini, released on November 20, 2026, went through about a trillion tokens of distillation after its experts were pruned, followed by reinforcement learning. That is typical: distillation first, then RL to sharpen the behaviours that matter.

The variants you will hear about: sequence-level distillation (train on the teacher's full answers), logit distillation (train on its probabilities, which needs access the public APIs do not give), self-distillation (a model teaching a smaller version of itself inside one lab), and reverse distillation, where a large model is trained on a smaller specialist's outputs to pick up a niche skill.

Why everyone does it

It is the cheapest route to a good small model. Pretraining a 25-billion-parameter model from scratch to frontier-small quality costs tens of millions of dollars in compute; distilling one from an existing frontier model costs a fraction of that, because the hard work of discovering what good answers look like has already been done. It is also why the flash, mini and nano tiers on the pricing tracker exist: vendors distil their own flagships into models that serve at a twentieth of the price, and the tier you pay $0.50 per million output tokens for is almost certainly a student of the one you pay $40 for.

For buyers, the practical consequence is that small models are very good at whatever their teacher was asked about and surprisingly poor at everything else. A distilled coding model may ace benchmarks and fall apart on a long multi-turn conversation, which is exactly the profile Inkling 2 Mini's own release notes describe.

Where the lines are

Two lines now matter. The first is contractual. Every major closed-model vendor forbids using its outputs to train a competing model, and OpenAI's usage policy that took effect on November 20, 2026 goes further: it bans using outputs to produce training signals of any kind for a substitute model, including grading rubrics, labels, reinforcement-learning environments and data filters, whether for yourself or for a client, and it bars extracting the hidden reasoning behind answers. Open-weight licences differ: Apache 2.0 and MIT models can be distilled freely, while Llama-style licences restrict using outputs to improve non-Llama models. The open-source licences explainer covers the differences.

The second line is security. Distillation needs prompts and answers at scale, and stolen ones are cheaper than bought ones. Railway's incident report in November 2026 found stolen API keys being used for distillation traffic within days, Google calls the pattern LLMjacking, and Dragos's canary study the same month showed a Chinese AI lab fetching data from prompts that had passed through third-party hosts. If you run an AI gateway, unusually uniform, high-volume, low-temperature traffic across many prompts is the signature to alert on.

What this means for you

If you buy models: expect small tiers to keep getting better and cheaper, because distillation improves as the teachers do, and test them on your own task distribution rather than the leaderboard. If you build models: read the terms of every API you call before an output lands in a training set, and keep a record of provenance, since the policy now covers indirect uses you may not think of as training. If you defend systems: your model keys are a distillation target, so spend caps, key scoping and traffic anomaly alerts are not optional. And if you are simply trying to understand the news: when a lab accuses another of distilling its models, the fight is about who paid for the hard part.

Common questions

Is distillation the same as fine-tuning?

No. Fine-tuning continues training an existing model on your examples to change its behaviour. Distillation creates or improves a smaller model using a larger model's outputs as the training data. You can fine-tune a distilled model, and many teams do.

Is distilling a model legal?

It depends on the source. Open-weight models under Apache 2.0 or MIT can be distilled freely. Closed APIs forbid it in their terms, and OpenAI's November 2026 policy also bans using outputs to build graders, labels or RL environments for substitute models. Breaking those terms risks losing access and, in some jurisdictions, contract and unfair-competition claims; this is not legal advice.

Can a distilled model be better than its teacher?

On a narrow task, yes: filtering the teacher's outputs for correctness before training, then adding reinforcement learning, can produce a student that beats the teacher on that task. Across the board, no; the student inherits the teacher's blind spots and adds its own.
