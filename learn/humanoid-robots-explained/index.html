Humanoid robots explained: what degrees of freedom, teleoperation and 'mass production' actually mean when a company shows you a robot on stage

Humanoid robots are legged, armed machines meant to do human work in human spaces. The demos are real and the economics are not settled; this is how to read the specifications, spot teleoperation, and tell a production date from a press release.

Updated 2026-11-25 · For: Beginners Executives ML engineers Finance

A humanoid robot is a machine with a torso, two arms and usually two legs, built to work in spaces designed for people: warehouses with shelves at human height, factory lines with human-spaced workstations, shops with counters. The argument for the shape is not that legs are efficient — wheels are better on flat floors — but that the world is already built around human bodies, so a human-shaped machine needs no new building.

The field went from research curiosity to a funded industry in about three years. Tesla's Optimus, Figure's humanoids, Agility's Digit, Apptronik's Apollo, Boston Dynamics' electric Atlas, Unitree and XPeng's IRON are the names that recur, with Chinese manufacturers raising at valuations that would have been unthinkable for a robot company in 2022. What has not happened yet is a fleet of them doing paid work without people nearby.

The specifications that mean something

Degrees of freedom (DOF) is the count of independently controllable joints. More DOF means more dexterity and more things to break, calibrate and power. Hand DOF is the number to look for: a humanoid with 6 DOF in each hand can grip and place; one with 20-plus can manipulate. Most useful work is hands.

Height and mass decide where it can legally work. A 170 cm, 62 kg robot is in the range where a fall is dangerous to a person standing next to it, which is why factory deployments start inside cages or in separated zones.

On-board compute is quoted in TOPS, trillions of operations per second. The figure tells you whether the control and perception stack runs locally or depends on a network link. A robot that needs the cloud to see is a robot that stops when the Wi-Fi does.

Battery runtime is the specification companies skip. Two hours of work and an hour of charging is three shifts of coverage for every four robots you buy, and that arithmetic belongs in the business case rather than the demo.

How to watch a demo

Ask four questions. Is anyone teleoperating? Remote human control, sometimes via a motion-capture suit, produces the fluid movements that go viral, and it is legitimate for data collection — it is how several companies generate their training sets — but it is not autonomy. Reputable presenters now say which it is.

Is the task one specific sequence or a class of tasks? Folding one particular shirt on one particular table is a rehearsed routine. Folding whatever is in the basket is a capability.

How many takes? Published success rates over many trials beat one clean run. The honest metric is attempts per success on a task the robot has not seen staged.

What happens when something moves unexpectedly? The hard problem in the real world is not the task, it is the person who walks into frame.

'Mass production' and other dates

Robot announcements carry three different dates that get reported as one. Hardware mass production means the line is making units — possibly for internal use, pilots and demonstrations. Commercial launch means a customer can buy one, and often means a letter of intent rather than a purchase order. General availability means anyone can order at a published price, which almost no humanoid has reached. When a company says production starts in January and launch is in the second quarter, those are different commitments, and only the second has a price attached.

The business model to watch is robots-as-a-service: the customer pays per robot per month rather than buying, which keeps maintenance and software updates with the manufacturer and keeps the capital cost off the customer's balance sheet. Most announced humanoid deals are structured this way, which is also why announced unit numbers are often ceilings rather than orders.

Where this is genuinely working

Structured, repetitive, physically unpleasant tasks in logistics: moving totes, unloading trailers, machine tending. The jobs are hard to fill, the environment is controlled, and the task tolerates a slow robot. Everything else — domestic work, elder care, retail service — is a research programme with a marketing budget. The gap between the two is not intelligence. It is reliability over thousands of hours in a space you do not control.

Common questions

Why humanoid shape instead of a wheeled robot with arms?

Because the environment is already built for human bodies: stairs, door handles, shelf heights, workstation spacing. A wheeled base is cheaper, more stable and more efficient wherever the floor is flat and clear, which is why most robots actually doing paid work today are wheeled. The humanoid bet is that retrofitting buildings costs more than legs.

Is the robot in the video being remote-controlled?

Often, and that is not automatically dishonest — teleoperation is a standard way to collect training data. The test is whether the company says so. Look for the phrase 'without teleoperation' or 'fully autonomous', the presence of a human in a motion-capture rig off-camera, and whether the robot handles an unscripted interruption. If a presenter will not answer the question directly, assume a human is in the loop.

When will these be cheap enough to matter?

Nobody has published a credible unit cost at volume, and the component that dominates it — actuators with high torque density — does not fall in price the way chips do. The honest answer is that per-month service pricing will arrive before a purchase price does, because it lets the manufacturer amortise hardware it cannot yet build cheaply. Treat any single-unit price quoted today as a marketing number.
