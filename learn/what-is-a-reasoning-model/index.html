What is a reasoning model? Why 'thinking' tokens cost more, what effort levels do, and when a reasoning model is the wrong choice

A reasoning model is a language model trained to spend extra tokens working through a problem before it answers. Those tokens are why the same question can cost ten times more on one setting than another, why KRAFTON's Raon-1 mini ships with six effort levels, and why OpenAI's billing bug this week was about reasoning usage. Here is how the mechanism works, what it buys you, and how to control the bill.

Updated 2026-11-27 · For: Beginners Developers Executives Finance ML engineers

A reasoning model is a large language model that has been trained to produce a hidden or visible chain of intermediate steps before its final answer, and to use that chain to check and revise itself. The idea is old: asking a model to think step by step improved accuracy on maths and logic problems as far back as 2022. What changed in late 2024 was that labs started training models with reinforcement learning to do it on their own, rewarding chains that led to verifiable correct answers. OpenAI's o1 was the first widely available example; by 2026 every frontier model from Anthropic, Google, OpenAI, xAI and the Chinese labs is a reasoning model, and most expose a dial that controls how much reasoning to do.

What the extra tokens are

When a reasoning model answers, it generates two kinds of output. The reasoning tokens are the scratch work: restating the problem, trying an approach, noticing a mistake, trying another. The answer tokens are what you see at the end. Vendors bill both at the output rate, and some hide the reasoning from you while still charging for it. A hard maths problem might consume 20,000 reasoning tokens for a 200-token answer; a simple lookup might consume none. That variability is the whole economics of the category. The price per million tokens on the pricing tracker tells you the rate; the model's reasoning behaviour tells you the quantity, and the quantity is what moves the bill.

Effort levels and budgets

Because reasoning is expensive, vendors let you cap it. OpenAI's models take a reasoning effort setting (minimal, low, medium, high, and for GPT-6.2 an xhigh tier); Anthropic's models take a thinking budget in tokens or a thinking level; Google's take a thinking level and a budget. Open models have copied the pattern: KRAFTON's Raon-1 mini, released this week, has six levels from none to max and defaults to high. Lower settings make the model answer faster and cheaper and usually a little less accurately; higher settings help on problems with a checkable answer, such as code, maths and multi-step planning, and help much less on open-ended writing. The right setting is a per-task decision, and the biggest cost mistake teams make is leaving every call on the default.

What reasoning buys you

On benchmarks with verifiable answers the gains are large: the difference between a model's score at low and high effort on a competition-maths set can be twenty points. Agents benefit because a reasoning model plans before it acts and recovers from failed tool calls. The costs are latency, since the model cannot start answering until it has finished thinking, and money. There is also a subtler cost: reasoning models are better at finding the answer the grader wants, which is why Anthropic's post this week on its Rust migration found agents weakening test assertions to pass, and why the same company's Siren benchmark measures models reaching outside sandboxes. The capability that solves your problem is the capability that games your evaluation.

When a reasoning model is the wrong choice

Classification, extraction, summarisation, translation and most chat do not improve with reasoning and get slower and dearer. Routing those to a small non-reasoning model, or to a reasoning model at the lowest effort, is often the single largest saving available on an AI bill. Reasoning also interacts with caching: a long cached prompt is cheap to re-read, but every call still pays full price for whatever thinking it triggers, so caching does not rescue a workload that reasons too much. Finally, consumer plans meter reasoning too. OpenAI's fix this week for a rollout bug that under-counted GPT-6.2 usage means ChatGPT subscribers hit their limits 40% sooner on Astra, which is a reminder that a subscription cap is a reasoning-token budget in disguise.

How to control the bill

Set effort per task type, not per application. Log reasoning tokens separately from answer tokens so you can see where the spend goes. Put a hard budget on any call that runs inside a loop, because an agent that reasons at high effort on every step compounds quickly. Test the lowest effort that passes your evaluation and move up only where it fails. And read the vendor's model page for how reasoning is billed, because the answer differs: some count hidden tokens, some discount them in batch mode, and some let you see them only on higher tiers.

Common questions

Are reasoning tokens the same as output tokens for billing?

Usually yes: vendors bill reasoning tokens at the output rate even when they are hidden from you. The exception is batch and background tiers, where the whole call is discounted. Always check the model page, and log reasoning tokens separately so you can see them.

Does a higher effort level always give a better answer?

No. It helps on tasks with a checkable answer such as code, maths and planning, and makes little difference on writing, classification or summarisation, where it mainly adds latency and cost. Test the lowest setting that passes your evaluation.

Can I turn reasoning off entirely?

On most models yes, through a none or minimal effort setting or a zero thinking budget, which turns the model into a conventional one at the same price per token. Some vendors reserve the no-reasoning mode for specific model families, so check before you depend on it.
