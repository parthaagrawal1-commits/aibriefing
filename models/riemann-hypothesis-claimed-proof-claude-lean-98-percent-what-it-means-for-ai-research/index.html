DevelopingAn Anthropic mathematician says he and Claude proved the Riemann hypothesis, released only as Lean code; James Maynard puts it at '98 or 99 percent'. What has been checked, what has not, and why labs are now talking about limits

A four-page note, a machine-checked proof nobody has yet read as prose, and a Navier–Stokes claim on the same day have pushed 'superintelligence' from marketing into the mouths of chief scientists.

Why it matters
ML engineersThe artefact is a Lean formalisation that compiles; the open questions are whether the formal statement is exactly RH or GRH, whether any axioms beyond Lean's standard library were used, and whether anyone can extract a human-readable argument. Those are checks you can run, and they are the new standard for any AI-generated proof claim.
ExecutivesWhatever the verdict, three labs' leaders spent the weekend arguing about whether to slow down. That debate will reach your regulators and your board before it reaches your product roadmap.
BeginnersThe Riemann hypothesis is a 167-year-old statement about prime numbers with a $1 million prize attached. A proof checked by software is strong evidence, but mathematicians also want to understand why it works, and that part has not happened yet.

On Friday afternoon, one day after Anthropic's shares began trading, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician employed by the company, posted a four-page note claiming a proof of the Riemann hypothesis developed with Claude. The note says the method also settles the generalised version, GRH, and includes a timeline compiled by Claude running from October 6 to November 16. What Alpöge released is not a paper. It is Lean code, a formal proof that a computer can check line by line, and a footnote says his own prose draft ran to roughly 250,000 pages. He denies the timing had anything to do with the IPO, and points out he used Claude in July to find a counterexample to a conjecture about Jacobians.

What has been checked

By Friday evening Andrew Sutherland at MIT had compiled the Lean independently, meaning the proof type-checks. The remaining mechanical step is an automated comparison confirming that the theorem as formalised is actually the Riemann hypothesis and not a subtly weaker statement; Elliot Glazer, who works on formal mathematics, said the formalised statement is in fact GRH. James Maynard, the Oxford number theorist and Fields medallist, told the New York Times he was 98 or 99 percent sure it was correct and that it would be a historic milestone if so, while warning that understanding why it works will take a lot of work. Terence Tao wrote that comprehension will take time. Brian Conrad at Stanford called the manner of release frankly completely outrageous, which is the sharpest expression of a widespread complaint: a proof nobody can read is not yet mathematics in the usual sense, even if a machine says it is true.

The same day, Tristan Buckmaster of NYU and Alpöge posted a claimed proof of finite-time blow-up for the unforced three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations, another Millennium Prize problem, with a Lean package of about 600,000 lines. Buckmaster called the write-up unpolished and heavily LLM-assisted and said that after OpenAI's September claim on the forced case they could not risk waiting. A caveat applies: the numerical certificate the formal theorems depend on has not been released, only its hash.

The reaction that matters for the industry

Mathematicians will take months. The AI industry took a weekend. Alexandr Wang, Meta's chief AI officer, posted on Sunday that superintelligence is arriving and that humanity should not accept a handover of society to AI. Elon Musk declared the singularity. More substantively, OpenAI's chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published an essay on Sunday arguing that pretraining perplexity, the model's error at predicting the next token, is a more durable measure of capability than saturating benchmarks, and proposing that labs commit not to train past a shared perplexity threshold until monitoring and understanding reach a concrete milestone. Sam Altman called it the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while. METR's quarterly update, released Friday, said the length of tasks models can complete is doubling roughly every 3.6 months and that public evidence cannot rule out a sharp acceleration in AI research itself.

The counter-argument is worth stating. A formally verified proof of a famous theorem is a capability demonstration, not evidence of autonomy; Alpöge drove the process for six weeks. Perplexity thresholds are easy to propose and hard to verify across labs with different tokenisers and data. And the loudest voices on Sunday all have products to sell.

What to watch

Three checks will settle the mathematics: the statement-equivalence check Sutherland was running, an audit of the Lean project for non-standard axioms or unproved assumptions, and the first human-readable extraction of the argument's core idea. For Navier–Stokes, the release of the numerical certificate. For the industry, whether any second lab endorses Pachocki's threshold idea in writing, and whether the perplexity numbers he cited, around 0.91 for GPT-4 and below 0.8 for today's frontier, get published with methodology. If they do, the site's benchmark explainer will need a new section.

Key terms in this story

  • Perplexity A measure of how surprised a model is by text it has not seen; lower means better next-token prediction.
  • Benchmark A standard test used to compare models, such as a set of coding tasks or exam questions.
  • Token The unit models read and write; roughly three-quarters of an English word. Pricing and context limits are counted in tokens.
  • Lean (proof assistant) A programming language and checker in which a mathematical proof is written as code that a computer verifies line by line.
