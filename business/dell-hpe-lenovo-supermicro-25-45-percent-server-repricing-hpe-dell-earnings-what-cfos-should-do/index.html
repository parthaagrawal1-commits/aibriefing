DevelopingDell, HPE, Lenovo and Supermicro are reportedly asking 25–45% above signed prices on AI servers at delivery, with HPE reporting tonight and Dell tomorrow. What CFOs should do about a fixed-price contract that is not

Memory is now more than half of a server's bill of materials and the vendors have rewritten their quoting terms to pass the increase on; one hedge fund is already suing over a $549 million order.

Why it matters
FinanceAny AI hardware line in your 2027 budget quoted before autumn is now a range, not a number. Pull the purchase orders and read the price-adjustment clause before the vendor does; where there is none, you have leverage, and where there is one, you have a forecast revision to make this quarter.
ExecutivesThe surcharge is the market telling you that compute is scarce in a way software never was. Decide now whether to accept delivery at the higher price, delay, or shift workloads to cloud and lease arrangements that absorb the component risk for a fee.
DevelopersHardware delays and repricing push teams toward smaller models and better inference efficiency. Measure tokens per dollar on what you already run before anyone approves a new rack.

The Financial Times reported over the weekend that Dell, Hewlett Packard Enterprise, Lenovo and Supermicro are seeking 25% to 45% more than the prices in signed contracts when Nvidia-based AI servers reach delivery, citing shortages of memory and printed circuit boards. HPE's chief executive, Antonio Neri, has told investors the company changed its quoting terms so that it can reprice an order for commodity cost increases between quote and shipment, and that he expects memory and storage prices to stay high well into 2027. Memory, he said, is now more than half of a server's bill of materials. The report's sharpest example is the hedge fund XTX, which paid Dell about $5 million extra for price certainty on a $549 million order and is suing in Ireland after Dell sought a further $62 million to $70 million.

We have not been able to read the FT article directly tonight and are relying on summaries of it; treat the precise percentages as the FT's reporting rather than company statements. Neri's remarks are consistent with what HPE said on its last earnings call.

Why this is happening now

AI servers are unusual products: they are quoted months ahead, built from components whose prices move weekly, and bought in quantities large enough to move those prices. High-bandwidth memory and DDR5 have been in short supply all year as every hyperscaler's build-out hits the same suppliers. Server makers used to absorb the swings inside margins of a few points. At a 40% move in the dominant component, that is impossible, and the vendors have chosen to change the contract rather than eat the loss. It is the same story behind Nvidia's own $390 billion of purchase commitments disclosed last week: everyone is locking in supply, and the cost of doing so is rising.

The buyers' counter-argument is simple. A signed price is a signed price, and a company that wanted flexibility should have quoted it. XTX's lawsuit will test that in court; most customers will test it in negotiation, where the vendor's leverage is that the alternative supplier faces the same memory bill.

This week's earnings are the tell

HPE reports after the market closes today. Dell, HP Inc, Workday, Autodesk and Nutanix report on Tuesday afternoon, Analog Devices on Tuesday morning. Dell and HPE between them will show how much AI server backlog is converting to revenue, what gross margin it carries, and whether the repricing is being accepted. Friday's 5.3% fall in the Nasdaq makes the margin line matter more than the backlog line: a company shipping record AI servers at 10% gross margin is not the growth story the stock price assumed. Listen for three phrases: price adjustment, memory cost pass-through, and cancellations.

What to do

For finance teams, start with the paperwork. Every open AI hardware order should be sorted into three piles: fixed price with no adjustment clause, which you can hold the vendor to; adjustable, which needs a revised forecast now; and not yet signed, where you should insist on a cap or a component-indexed formula rather than open-ended repricing. Then compare the adjusted on-premises cost with cloud and lease alternatives, where providers carry the component risk and charge for it; after this week that charge may look cheap. If you are mid-delivery, ask whether a partial acceptance at the original price and a renegotiated remainder is on offer; vendors want the revenue recognised this quarter as much as you want the hardware.

For technology leaders, the practical response is efficiency. Smaller models, better batching, prompt caching and quantised deployments all reduce how many of these servers you need, and the pricing tracker on this site shows frontier-class output tokens now available from several vendors at a fifth of what they cost a year ago. The hardware you do not have to buy is the only line item immune to a 45% surcharge.
