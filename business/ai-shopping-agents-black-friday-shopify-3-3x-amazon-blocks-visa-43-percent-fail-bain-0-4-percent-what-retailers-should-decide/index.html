The first agentic Black Friday by the numbers: 3.3x growth at Shopify, 0.4% of e-commerce, a 43% failure rate, and a wall of retailer blocks

Agents are growing fast from almost nothing and failing almost half the time; the retailers deciding whether to let them in are choosing between a new sales channel and the loss of their first-party data and ad revenue.

Why it matters
ExecutivesDecide your agent policy before Q1: allow browsing only, allow checkout through a merchant API, or block. Not deciding means the bot-blocker vendor decides for you.
FinanceAmazon blocks agents to defend sponsored-product revenue and first-party data. Shopify welcomes them because it sells to merchants, not shoppers. Follow the incentive, not the press release.
SecurityAn agent that checks out holds a payment credential. Visa's 43% failure rate is your chargeback forecast; use network tokens with per-agent limits, never a stored card.

Shopify's president, Harley Finkelstein, told CNBC on Friday that this was the first Black Friday on which AI agents played a real role. The platform's checkout peaked at roughly $2.3 million a minute, and sales that originated from an agent, meaning a shopper's assistant found or recommended the product, ran about 3.3 times last year's level. Shopify did not say what share of total sales that represented, which is the tell: the base is small.

The rest of the market spent the day keeping agents out. Amazon has blocked Meta's Muse since September, when it said it had never agreed to join the program; it stopped xAI's Grok Bot from placing orders last month and is suing Perplexity over the shopping agent in its Comet browser, with a trial due next year. eBay, United Airlines and Kohl's limit what agents can do. Coach and Gap allow browsing but not checkout. JPMorgan Chase, Charles Schwab and Neiman Marcus have moved to block Muse outright. Meta's own figures, as Mark Zuckerberg put them in October, are that about a quarter of Muse's 10 million users have shopped through it, spending about $200 per order.

The three numbers

Bain estimates that agent-initiated purchases account for 0.4% of global e-commerce transactions and 0.2% of their value: shoppers let agents buy cheap things. Visa, which has the most to gain from agentic payments and has built tokenised credentials for them, found in its own tests that agents failed 43% of shopping tasks. And 23% of US consumers say they would trust an AI to pay on their behalf.

Put together, the picture is coherent. Adoption is growing several-fold from a base near zero, reliability is well short of what a human expects from a cashier, and trust tracks reliability. The 3.3x figure and the 0.4% figure are the same story told by a seller of tools and a consultancy, respectively.

Why retailers block

The stated reasons are customer safety and terms of service. The structural reasons are revenue and data. Amazon's advertising business depends on shoppers seeing sponsored placements; an agent that queries the catalogue and returns the best price does not see ads. First-party browsing data, the input to recommendations and to ad targeting, is lost when the shopper never visits. For a marketplace, an agent is a price-comparison engine with a credit card, and the history of price-comparison engines is that marketplaces fought them until they could not.

Shopify's position is the mirror image. It sells infrastructure to merchants and earns on transaction volume; an agent that brings a buyer to a Shopify store is a free acquisition channel. Its agentic checkout, which exposes a structured API rather than a web page, is the model that agent builders can rely on, and the one that lets a merchant decide what an agent may do.

The counter-argument to blocking is simple: shoppers who want to use agents will go where agents work. If Shopify merchants capture the 3.3x growth and Amazon captures none of it, the 0.4% share will not stay at 0.4% for the one side.

What to decide

  • Browse, buy, or block. Three policies, each defensible. The Coach-and-Gap position, browse but not checkout, keeps the shopper on your property for the transaction and loses nothing to bots. Choose explicitly and write it into robots.txt, terms and bot-management rules so they agree.
  • Expose a structured surface. If you allow agents at all, give them a product feed and a checkout API rather than letting them scrape. Scraped agents fail 43% of the time; API-driven ones fail at the rate of your API.
  • Payments. Insist on network tokens scoped to the agent and the merchant, with spending caps. A stored card number in an agent's memory is a breach waiting for a prompt injection.
  • Measure attribution. Shopify can report agent-sourced sales because it instrumented them. If you cannot tell an agent from a shopper in your analytics by Q1, you will not know whether the channel is worth the risk.
  • Watch the Perplexity case. Amazon v. Perplexity will decide whether a browser agent acting for a logged-in user is the user or an unauthorised bot. That ruling, not Black Friday, sets the terms for 2027.

Key terms in this story

  • Prompt injection An attack in which text the model reads, such as a web page or email, contains instructions that hijack its behaviour.
  • Network token (payments) A substitute card number issued by Visa, Mastercard or American Express for one transaction or merchant, so the real card details never reach the merchant or the software making the purchase.
  • Perplexity A measure of how surprised a model is by text it has not seen; lower means better next-token prediction.
  • Token The unit models read and write; roughly three-quarters of an English word. Pricing and context limits are counted in tokens.
  • Agent An AI system that pursues a goal by taking actions with tools, in a loop, rather than answering a single question.
