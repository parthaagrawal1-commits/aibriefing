DevelopingSwitzerland says AI agents breached four of five targets, and Microsoft says Salt Typhoon now runs ARTEX: the two reports read as one

A national cyber office has told operators to patch internet-facing systems within 24 hours because agent attacks succeed against weak passwords and known flaws by default; Microsoft's list of ARTEX users explains why.

Why it matters
Security24-hour patching for the internet-facing subset, passkeys with no exceptions on webmail and VPN, and burst-rate detection are the three controls both reports point to.
ExecutivesA regulator has said these attacks succeed by default. Ask your CISO for the list of internet-facing systems and the current time-to-patch for each.

Switzerland's Federal Office for Cybersecurity (BACS) published a bulletin on November 24 saying that in every case known to it, the use of AI agents by attackers had led to a successful compromise, and that it expects more. Its director, Florian Schütz, put numbers on it in an SRF interview reported on Thursday: five attacks reported, three against operators of critical infrastructure in sectors such as electricity, drinking water and telecoms, and four of the five successful, with no major disruption. Victims, some of them small and medium-sized firms that reported voluntarily, are not named, and BACS has not attributed the attacks. Operators of critical infrastructure have had to report incidents within 24 hours since April 2025, so the count reflects what was reported, not what happened.

What the agents did

BACS describes agents that find vulnerabilities on their own, choose tools, assess results and plan next steps, and says they mainly exploited weak or guessed passwords and known flaws in internet-facing software. That is the same tradecraft Microsoft documented on November 25 in its report on ARTEX, the open-source agentic pentest framework published on GitHub in July. Microsoft names six groups that have integrated it: China's Salt Typhoon and Crystal Typhoon, North Korea's Onyx Sleet and Pearl Sleet, and Storm-0940 and Storm-3358. In one Storm-0940 password spray against Microsoft 365, more than 151,000 failed sign-ins were logged at a peak of 2,900 a minute, and privileged access followed in under three hours; the same group is tied to the ConnectWise supply-chain compromise. Microsoft describes a planner agent that sets goals, worker agents with a real shell, and a reporter agent, and says the framework runs on almost any model, including ones with guardrails removed.

BACS cites ARTEX and CyberStrikeAI as examples of agents reachable on the internet rather than as the tools used in the five cases; the FBI's November flash put exposed ARTEX consoles above 1,600. Italy's CSIRT issued a parallel bulletin naming PentAGI and DeepSeek Harness as well, and the UK's NCSC reports a marked rise in AI-assisted attacks since September.

The 24-hour rule

The Swiss recommendations are notable for how tight they are. Patch critical internet-facing systems within 24 hours of a release. Require MFA or passkeys with no exceptions on webmail and VPN. Reduce the number of internet-facing systems and restrict access with geo-blocking and allow lists. Grant the lowest privileges that work. Monitor for unusual behaviour and data volumes across identities, applications and workloads. Run regular penetration tests. CISA's BOD 26-04 gives US federal agencies three days for the highest-risk flaws; the 2026 Verizon DBIR puts the median time to full remediation at 43 days. The gap between 24 hours and 43 days is the window the agents are working in.

The counter-argument is that these are five reports in a country of nine million people, that the sectors conflict between the bulletin and the interview, and that none of the cases caused an outage. That is fair, and BACS says so itself. But Microsoft's report is not about Switzerland and reaches the same conclusion from telemetry across more than 2,250 tenants: the attacks are not more sophisticated, they are faster, and speed is what breaks a 43-day patch cycle.

What to do

  • Build the list of internet-facing systems and commit to a 24-hour patch SLA for that list only. It is a smaller set than your whole estate and it is the set the agents scan.
  • Remove every MFA exception on webmail and VPN this week. Passkeys where you can; the Swiss wording is no exceptions, and the Storm-0940 spray is the reason.
  • Detect on behaviour, not indicators. Microsoft's guidance is that IPs and user agents rotate; what does not is the burst of varied requests from one source and the rapid jump between attack stages. Alert on both.
  • Deploy honeypots and tarpits. Microsoft recommends fake wins because agents, unlike humans, pursue them.
  • Rotate any secret that has ever been committed to code. The TechRadar account of Microsoft's report includes an agent that went from GitHub secrets to Azure Key Vault in five minutes.
  • Report incidents. The Swiss count exists because SMEs came forward voluntarily.
