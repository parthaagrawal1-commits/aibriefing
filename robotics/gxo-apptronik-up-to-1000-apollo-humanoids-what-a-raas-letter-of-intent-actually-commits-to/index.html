GXO's plan for up to 1,000 Apptronik Apollo humanoids over four years: what a robots-as-a-service letter of intent actually commits to, and what it does not

The largest pure-play contract logistics company has signed the biggest humanoid framework yet, but it is stepped, KPI-gated and paid per robot-hour; three months ago its CEO said humanoids had not yet returned their cost, which makes this a test the whole industry will be graded on.

Why it matters
ExecutivesThe structure is the lesson: no fleet purchase, a letter of intent not a contract, expanded trials before any commercial unit, and scale that depends on jointly agreed performance, safety and operational KPIs per use case. Copy that shape before you sign anything with a humanoid vendor.
FinanceGXO has 45 pilots behind it and said in August that ROI on humanoids was 'a couple of years away'. The deal does not change that timeline; first commercial deployments are late 2027. The signal is that the buyer is willing to pay for the option.
ML engineersThe task list is picking, case and tote movement, packing and sortation, with hand dexterity and speed named as the bottlenecks. That matches J.P. Morgan's bottleneck analysis last week: hands and the brain, not the body.

GXO Logistics and Apptronik announced on November 12 a letter of intent under which Austin-based Apptronik will supply up to 1,000 Apollo humanoid robots to GXO facilities worldwide over four years. Expanded trials begin in 2027 and the first commercial deployments are planned for North America in late 2027. The commercial model is robots-as-a-service: GXO pays for robot capacity rather than buying a fleet, and the rollout proceeds in steps, with each use case validated against jointly agreed performance, safety and operational KPIs before it scales. The two companies have worked together since 2024 and have run data collection and live autonomous evaluations on more than 50 logistics tasks at Apptronik's 90,000 square foot Robot Park, after an earlier US pilot in which Apollo picked small consumer goods autonomously.

What the number means

One thousand is a ceiling, not an order. GXO runs more than 1,000 facilities and 200 million square feet, employs over 150,000 people and is putting roughly 20,000 conventional robots to work across its network this year, so even a full rollout would be one humanoid per site against twenty wheeled or arm robots. The company has run more than 45 humanoid pilots and was Agility Robotics' first commercial customer in June 2024, has since signed a second humanoid RaaS deal with Reflex Robotics, and is expanding Agility's Digit to two more warehouses in Statesville, North Carolina and Lebanon, Tennessee from early 2027. Apptronik's Apollo is being pointed at a wider task set than Digit's tote handling: goods-to-person and person-to-goods picking, case and tote movement, packing and sortation. CEO Patrick Kelleher said on the August earnings call that GXO had not achieved ROI on humanoids yet and was a couple of years away, naming hand dexterity and speed as the hurdles. Nothing in the November announcement moves that date; it formalises the path to it.

Why the structure matters more than the headline

Humanoid announcements have mostly been counted in units promised. This one is instructive because of what GXO did not commit to. There is no purchase, so the capital risk of a platform that is still improving sits with Apptronik, which raised $935 million this year and can carry it. There is no fixed delivery schedule, only a stepped expansion gated on KPIs, which lets GXO stop at any use case that fails. And the payment is for service, which means Apptronik is paid for robot-hours that do useful work, the metric investors have struggled to get from any humanoid vendor. J.P. Morgan's forecast last week of 300,000 to 400,000 US humanoids a year by 2030 named hands, actuators, the brain and manufacturing capacity as the bottlenecks; a 1,000-unit RaaS framework with a demanding operator is the kind of contract that produces the data to tell whether those bottlenecks are clearing.

The counter-argument

A letter of intent with a four-year horizon and a late-2027 first commercial deployment can be read as a press release about 2028. GXO gets to say it is the humanoid leader in logistics; Apptronik gets a marquee name ahead of its next raise; neither has disclosed pricing, uptime commitments or the KPIs. Sceptics will note that GXO's conventional robot count grows by tens of thousands a year while its humanoid count is still measured in pilots, and that OpenAI folded its standalone robotics team last week on the view that general models will subsume much of the robot-specific stack. The reply is that GXO is the operator with the most humanoid pilot data in the world, and it chose to go bigger, not smaller.

What to watch

Whether GXO discloses per-task throughput or cost-per-pick when expanded trials start in 2027, which would be the first operator-reported productivity figure for a humanoid at scale. Whether Agility, Reflex and Apptronik end up competing for the same GXO sites, which would turn RaaS into a price-and-performance contest. And the February earnings call, where Kelleher will be asked again whether the ROI timeline has moved.

Key terms in this story

  • Robots-as-a-service (RaaS) A commercial model in which a customer pays for robot capacity, usually per robot per month or per hour of work, instead of buying the hardware.
  • Evaluation (eval) A test set with known-good answers used to measure a model or system on your own tasks.
  • Degrees of freedom (robotics) The number of joints a robot can control independently; more means more dexterity and more to break, calibrate and power.
