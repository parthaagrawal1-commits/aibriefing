DevelopingTesla starts rolling out FSD v15 with a '25x better' safety claim and no baseline, the same weekend Waymo pulls its robotaxis off freeways nationwide. What the numbers do and do not show

One company shipped a new vision encoder shared with its humanoid and declared the singularity; the other accepted a California order after construction-zone incidents. Only one of them published a method.

Why it matters
ML engineersA 25x improvement in critical interventions is a ratio of two unpublished numbers over unpublished mileage. Until Tesla states the denominator, the comparable public datapoint remains the disengagement and incident reports regulators receive, which Waymo files and Tesla's consumer product does not.
ExecutivesThe shared encoder is the strategic detail: one perception model across cars, robotaxis and Optimus means the driving fleet's data now trains the robot. That is the real moat argument, and it is testable over the next year.
BeginnersBoth companies are being watched by California regulators. The difference this weekend is that Waymo was told to change behaviour and did, while Tesla's software is sold as driver assistance, so the driver stays responsible.

Tesla began pushing Full Self-Driving version 15 to early-access owners on Saturday, with Elon Musk promising a wide release in December's holiday update. The release notes describe a new shared vision encoder, trained on internet video and used across the consumer cars, the Robotaxi fleet and the Optimus humanoid; a generative overhead rendering Tesla calls Reality View; end-to-end learned handling of the start and end of trips; parking that remembers your usual spot; and better detection of signs, small animals, debris, potholes and standing water. Initial availability is on the redesigned Model Y; older HW3 cars are excluded.

The claim

The line everyone repeated is Tesla's: 25x better handling of safety-critical cases. On Sunday Musk clarified that he meant twenty-five times, not twenty-five percent, and welcomed readers to the singularity. What neither Tesla nor Musk has published is the baseline. The notes do not say whether the comparison is against v14, against an earlier build, or against a fleet average; they give no mileage, no definition of a critical case and no methodology. Reports describing the figure as 25x versus v14 are inferring, not citing. A ratio with no denominator is marketing until the denominator appears, and Tesla's consumer product is not subject to the disengagement reporting that California requires of permitted autonomous testers.

The technically interesting part is the encoder. If one perception model now serves cars, robotaxis and a humanoid, then every mile the fleet drives becomes training signal for the robot, which is the argument Tesla has been making for Optimus for two years. Musk said on Friday that Optimus would give an unscripted demonstration within one to two months and on Sunday that the AI5 chip would go to Optimus before the cars. Those are checkable promises with dates.

Waymo's weekend

Waymo, meanwhile, pulled its robotaxis off freeways across every market it operates in after an order from California regulators, following incidents in construction zones and closed freeway lanes. The company said no collisions or injuries resulted from the closed-lane events and that a recall remedy is pending. Freeway service was the feature Waymo rolled out most recently, and taking it away everywhere rather than only in California suggests the fix is in software rather than in local mapping. Elsewhere in the same roundup: Zoox robotaxis are now bookable through Uber in Las Vegas, Tesla has 1,507 robotaxis registered in Texas against Waymo's 1,345, and Tesla has opened personal Cybercab pre-orders at $29,990 plus $299 a month.

The counter-argument

Tesla's defenders will say that a disengagement count is a poor safety metric too, that Waymo's own numbers are self-reported, and that a company shipping to hundreds of thousands of cars has data no regulator-permitted fleet of a few thousand can match. That is partly true. It is also why the absence of any published figure is the story: the fleet with the most data has chosen to release a multiplier and nothing else.

What to watch

Three things in the next six weeks. Whether Tesla's wide release in December comes with any definition of a critical intervention or any mileage figure; whether Waymo's recall filing with NHTSA describes the construction-zone failure and the remedy, which will tell you how long freeway service stays off; and whether the Optimus demonstration Musk promised happens on the timeline he gave. For robotics teams, the shared-encoder claim is the one to probe: if Tesla publishes anything on transfer from driving video to manipulation, it will be the most consequential robotics paper of the quarter.
