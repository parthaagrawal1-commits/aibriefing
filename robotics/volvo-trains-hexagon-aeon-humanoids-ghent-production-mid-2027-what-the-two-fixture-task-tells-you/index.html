Volvo's humanoid pilot at Ghent starts with one task, two fixtures and a wheeled robot, which is exactly how a factory deployment should look

Hexagon's AEON is 1.65 m, 60 kg, rides on wheels and carries 10 kg; Volvo's own staff are teaching it by demonstration, with a larger pilot in early 2027 and production from mid-year.

Why it matters
ExecutivesThe scope is one ergonomically bad task on an existing line, trained by the plant's own people. That is the template to copy, not a general-purpose labour pitch.
FinanceNo unit count, no price, no productivity target. Hold Volvo and Hexagon to mid-2027 production, and ask what 'real production' means in takt time.
ML engineersImitation learning run by the customer keeps the demonstration data inside the plant. Ask who owns the resulting policy.

Volvo Cars said on Wednesday that it has begun training Hexagon Robotics' AEON humanoids at its plant in Ghent, Belgium, the first Volvo factory to try them. Volvo's own team, working with Hexagon's robotics division, is teaching the robots by demonstration to place components into two fixtures with high precision. Later stages add handling several parts at once, moving around the production area and interacting with the plant's automated systems. A larger pilot with more units follows in the first half of 2027, and Volvo wants the robots in real production from the middle of the year. Chief manufacturing officer Magnus Nilsson said the humanoid form lets AEON fit into production as it stands today.

The robot

AEON, unveiled by Hexagon in June 2025 and built on Nvidia's robotics stack, is 1.65 metres tall and 60 kg. Its articulated legs end in motorised wheels rather than feet, which gives it up to 2.4 m/s on a flat factory floor at the cost of stairs; it carries up to 10 kg and senses with cameras and the measurement hardware that is Hexagon's core business. The wheel decision is the tell. Humanoid vendors selling into factories are converging on the same conclusion: a torso and arms at human height are useful because the line was built for humans, but bipedal walking is expensive, slow and unnecessary on a surface that was poured flat. Agility's Digit and Figure's robots walk; Hexagon, 1X's industrial line and several Chinese entrants roll.

The task

Placing parts into two fixtures is the kind of job that gets a line worker a repetitive-strain claim: precise, repeated hundreds of times a shift, and physically awkward. It is also narrow enough to measure. Volvo frames the programme as supporting employees in repetitive and ergonomically challenging work, which is the framing the Belgian unions will hold it to. The sequence Volvo lays out, one task, then multi-part handling, then navigation, then integration with existing automation, is a maturity ladder, and each rung is a decision point to stop.

What Volvo has not said matters as much. There is no unit count on site or for the pilot, no price, no cycle-time target, and no statement of which vehicle line is involved; the EX30 is built at Ghent but neither company connects it to the robots. Imitation learning by the plant's own staff is the right approach for a task this specific, but it also means Volvo is generating the training data, and the announcement does not say who owns the resulting policy or whether it transfers to Hexagon's other customers.

The comparables

BMW has been piloting AEON at Leipzig, Schaeffler has committed to at least 1,000 units, Audi is testing mobile humanoids at Ingolstadt, and Siemens had a humanoid complete an eight-hour shift at Erlangen in April. Hyundai has opened an Atlas training centre at its Georgia plant with mass production planned from 2028, and GXO signed a letter of intent for up to 1,000 Apptronik Apollos this month. XPeng's IRON goes into hardware mass production in January. Against that list, Volvo's announcement is small, specific and dated, which is why it is more credible than most. The counter-argument is the one every humanoid story carries: a wheeled torso doing one fixture task is a mobile manipulator with a marketing name, and the automotive industry has run mobile-manipulator pilots for a decade without scaling them. The difference claimed now is that the policy is learned, not programmed, so the second task is cheaper than the first. Mid-2027 is when Volvo will have to show that.

What to watch

  • The first-half-2027 pilot: how many units, and whether the second task is announced with the same specificity as the first.
  • Takt time. 'Real production' means keeping pace with the line; Volvo has not said what cycle time the robots must hit.
  • Hexagon's order book. Schaeffler's 1,000-unit commitment and the BMW pilot are the volume; Volvo is the showcase.
  • Union terms at Ghent. Belgian works councils will want the ergonomics framing written into what the robots are allowed to replace.
