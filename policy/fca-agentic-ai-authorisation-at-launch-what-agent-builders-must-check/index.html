DevelopingThe FCA says some AI agents will need financial authorisation at launch, and calling yourself a tech company will not help

Britain's regulator published its agentic AI expectations on November 24: if your agent moves money, arranges credit or makes investment decisions, the perimeter already applies.

Why it matters
ExecutivesAccountability stays with the regulated firm and the named senior manager even when the agent is a third party's product. Outsourcing the model does not outsource the responsibility.
DevelopersSix activities to check against your product: payment services, financial promotions, authorised information services, credit broking, intermediation and advice. The FCA says Parliament set this boundary, which means the regulator cannot waive it for you.
SecurityThe FCA expects firms to be able to identify an AI agent, know what consents it holds and decide whether to accept payments from agent-controlled wallets. That is an identity and authorisation problem before it is a compliance one.
BeginnersIf an app's AI assistant can spend your money or take out a loan for you, UK regulators now say it may need the same licence a bank or broker needs.

The Financial Conduct Authority published "Our approach to Agentic AI" on November 24, aimed at regulated firms, technology providers and social media platforms. The core proposition is short: as AI moves from answering questions to initiating transactions, moving money, arranging borrowing and making investment decisions, firms need to work out which of those activities require authorisation. Some personal-agent products, the FCA says, will need authorisation at launch; others will need it later as they add capability. The page is explicit that the regulator is not drawing the line itself — "This boundary is set by Parliament."

For a company that has never thought of itself as a financial firm, the useful part is the checklist of what to test a product against: whether it provides payment services, issues financial promotions, operates as an authorised information service, performs credit broking, acts as an intermediary, or gives financial advice. An agent that compares mortgage deals and hands the user off is doing something with a name in UK law. So is one that posts a recommendation to buy a share.

Accountability does not move

The second half is addressed to the banks and brokers that will buy these agents. Responsibility stays with the regulated firm and with senior managers individually, including where the firm relies on a third-party provider, and a senior manager answers for models in their area whether built in-house or bought. Firms are expected to demonstrate they understand how autonomous the system is, what the risks are, and what controls exist in real time and under stress. The Consumer Duty applies to outcomes the agent produces.

The operational questions the FCA raises are the ones most firms cannot currently answer: how do you identify that the party on the other side of a transaction is an AI agent, what consents does it hold, what is it permitted to do, and will you accept payments from a wallet an agent controls? Those are the same questions the payments industry has spent a year arguing about, now with a supervisor's name attached.

The timing is not accidental

Four UK digital regulators issued a joint statement on personal AI agents in October, and on November 16 wrote to ministers warning that several such agents were about to launch in Britain. Meta's Muse arrived in the UK on November 23 with launch partners including Marks & Spencer, Ocado, Trainline and easyJet, and with Meta itself saying prompt injection "remains an open problem". A regulator publishing its expectations eight days later is not reacting to a hypothetical.

What the page does not contain is a consultation, a deadline or a rulebook change. The FCA promises regular updates, points firms to its AI Lab and pre-application support, and says it will run a dedicated "Agentic Policy Sprint" — with no date set. That is the gap: the perimeter applies now, by statute, but the guidance on how it applies to an agent will arrive on the regulator's schedule, and firms launching in the first half of 2027 will have to interpret it themselves.

What to do

Run the six-activity test against your product this quarter and write down the answer, because the FCA's position is that the question has an existing legal answer whether or not you have asked it. Name the senior manager accountable for each third-party model in use, and make sure that person can describe the agent's autonomy and its stress-case controls without calling the vendor. Decide your policy on agent-controlled wallets before a counterparty's agent presents one. If you are a technology provider planning a UK launch, use the FCA's pre-application route rather than launching and asking: authorisation at launch is the stated expectation, and retrofitting it after a consumer-harm event is the expensive path.

What to watch

A date for the Agentic Policy Sprint and whether it comes with a consultation paper; whether the FCA names a product publicly, which would turn guidance into enforcement signal; how the EU and the US respond, since an agent that needs a licence in London and not in Dublin is an arbitrage the industry will find quickly; and whether any of the newly launched personal agents in Britain applies for authorisation rather than arguing it is out of scope.
