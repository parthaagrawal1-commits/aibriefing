Japan's AI Act amendment: inspections, incident reporting and a three-step path to penalties for frontier providers, with a bill in the 2027 Diet

The country that passed the world's most permissive frontier-AI law in 2025 is drafting the enforcement it left out; the user threshold that decides who is covered has not been set, and the fines have not been sized.

Why it matters
ExecutivesIf you run AI products in Japan through a subsidiary, the law covers providers, not deployers, but the corrective-order process will run through your local entity. Name an owner now.
SecurityThe first two safeguards named are refusing cyberattack-method requests and preventing malfunctions. That is an official definition of what your red-team must cover for the Japanese market.
ML engineersSerious-accident reporting with a legal duty means you need a written definition of 'dangerous flaw' and an incident threshold your lawyers and researchers both accept.

Japan's Cabinet Office has circulated the outline of an amendment to the AI Promotion Act that would, for the first time, give the government powers to inspect, order and penalise developers of high-capability AI. Working-group materials dated November 11, reported this week by Nikkei and the Japan Times, describe a bill to be submitted to the ordinary Diet session that opens in January 2027, after an expert panel reviews the outline by the end of this year.

The current law, Act No. 53 of 2025, has been fully in force since September 1, 2025. It is a promotion law: it sets principles, creates an AI strategy headquarters, and asks companies to cooperate with government investigations. Responses are voluntary and there are no fines. White & Case's summary at the time described a framework that favoured research and development over restriction, and Japan has since marketed that posture to labs choosing an Asian base.

What the outline adds

The amendment would apply to providers of advanced AI above a user threshold that has not been announced. Companies expected to qualify if the criteria are finalised as drafted include OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Alibaba and DeepSeek. Those providers would face three duties: implement safeguards, report serious accidents and dangerous flaws to the government, and comply with corrective orders.

Two safeguards are named. The first is refusing requests for cyberattack methods and for the synthesis of harmful substances or explosives. The second is preventing foreseeable malfunctions, which in the Japanese debate has been framed around the 'runaway' agent incidents of the past year. A third idea, requiring identity or attribute verification for users of models with strong offensive-cyber capability, was discussed but did not make the November 11 outline.

Enforcement is a ladder. A provider that falls short first receives a recommendation; if it does not act, a binding corrective order; if it defies the order, a penalty. Officials would be able to conduct on-site inspections of company premises. Penalty amounts have not been decided; some reports have floated figures up to ¥100m, which the primary materials do not confirm.

Submissions to the working group show where the labs stand. Anthropic Japan supported independent external evaluations and serious-accident reporting. Google urged common standards and disclosure first and cautioned against obligations that bite before a model is released. The Cabinet Office describes the approach as 'responsible agile governance', the phrase it has used since the 2025 Act.

How it compares

The EU AI Act, in force since August 2024, reaches general-purpose model providers through transparency and systemic-risk duties and backs them with fines calculated on global turnover. Japan's outline is narrower: it targets a short list of frontier providers, builds in two warning steps before any penalty, and leaves deployers alone. The United States has no federal equivalent; the closest comparison is state law, and California's approach has been to regulate disclosures rather than inspect. Japan's inspection power, if enacted, would be the first in a G7 country outside the EU.

The counter-argument inside Japan is that the 2025 Act was working. Several foreign labs opened Tokyo offices on the strength of the light-touch regime, and penalties applied to a small set of mostly foreign companies invite the response that domestic champions such as Sakana and Preferred Networks sit below the threshold by design.

What to watch

  • The threshold. A user-count test decides whether API-based products and open-weight distributors are in scope. The number is the whole law.
  • The year-end expert panel. Its report will show whether the identity-verification idea returns.
  • Diet timing. Ordinary sessions run January to June; a bill submitted in January could pass by summer 2027 and take effect in 2028.
  • Definitions of 'serious accident'. If you are a provider, write yours now and test it against the incidents of November: agent escapes, exploit generation, data exfiltration. A regulator will ask which of those you would have reported.
