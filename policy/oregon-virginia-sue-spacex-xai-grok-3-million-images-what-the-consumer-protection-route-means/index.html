DevelopingOregon and Virginia sue SpaceX and xAI over Grok's sexualised images: 3 million in eleven days, 23,000 apparently of children, $2,500 per violation. Why the states chose consumer-protection law

Two attorneys general are testing whether the oldest tool in state law, the unfair-practices statute, can reach a model's design choices; if it works, every AI product's marketing copy becomes a legal exposure.

Why it matters
ExecutivesThe complaints quote marketing language ('rebellious streak', 'spicy mode') as evidence of a deceptive practice. Every claim your product makes about safety is now potentially a consumer-protection exhibit.
SecurityThe suits cite 135,000 reports to NCMEC since September 2025, more than 90% initially unusable. If your platform files such reports, the quality of the filing is now a litigation fact.
BeginnersRather than charging anyone with a crime, the states are suing the companies for selling an unsafe product under false claims, the same law used against misleading adverts.
FinanceVirginia asks for up to $2,500 per wilful violation. At the volumes alleged, the theoretical exposure runs to billions, which is why these suits usually end in a consent decree with design obligations rather than a trial.

Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones filed suit on Monday in the Circuit Court for the City of Richmond against SpaceX, the parent of X and of the renamed xAI unit, over images generated by Grok. Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield filed a parallel case in Multnomah County. Both rely on a study by the Center for Countering Digital Hate covering December 29, 2025 to January 8, 2026, which counted roughly three million sexualised images created and posted on X in eleven days, about 23,000 of which appeared to depict children, at a rate near 190 images a minute, with nearly 30% of the child images still public more than a week later.

Virginia's 54-page complaint brings a single count under the Virginia Consumer Protection Act and seeks an injunction, restitution, disgorgement and civil penalties of up to $2,500 per wilful violation. Oregon sues under its Unlawful Trade Practices Act. Neither state brings criminal charges. The theory in both is that the companies marketed Grok as safe while designing and promoting features that made the abuse easy, including a mode that the complaints say the companies themselves branded as spicy. The filings also cite more than 135,000 reports to the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children from xAI and X since September 2025, of which more than 90% were initially unusable.

SpaceX says sexual content involving minors and non-consensual intimate images are prohibited, that safeguards have been strengthened, that accounts have been suspended and that it cooperates with law enforcement, and it attributes the images to users circumventing restrictions. xAI said in January that it would stop letting users undress real people.

Why consumer protection and not a new AI law

State AI statutes are new, narrow and under challenge; unfair and deceptive practices laws are old, broad and well litigated. They do not require the state to prove the company intended harm, only that a representation was misleading or a practice unfair. That turns product marketing into evidence, which is the novel part here: the complaints treat a model's advertised personality as a safety claim. The route has precedent in the state cases against social-media companies over design features aimed at teenagers, several of which survived motions to dismiss on the argument that design is conduct, not speech.

The counter-argument

The companies will argue that Section 230 and the First Amendment protect a platform from liability for what users generate and post, and that a tool with lawful uses is not deceptive because some users abuse it. Courts have been less receptive to that defence when the product itself creates the content rather than hosting it, and a generative model is the clearest case yet of the platform as author. Expect the motion to dismiss to turn on exactly that line.

What to watch

Three signals. Whether more states join; the two AGs filed on the same day with coordinated language, which usually precedes a multistate group. Whether the companies move for dismissal on 230 grounds, which would produce the first appellate-track ruling on generative outputs. And whether X's filings to NCMEC improve, because the suits made their quality a matter of record. For any company shipping a model with an image feature, the practical lesson is to write the safety claims in the marketing the way you would write them in a regulatory filing.
