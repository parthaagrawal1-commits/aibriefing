DevelopingThe FAA grounded every US departure for five hours, three days after its $875 million SMART AI tool went to all 22 centres: what is known, what is not

Officials blame legacy telecom hardware and the vendor denies any link, but the homeland security secretary would not clear the system, and the episode is a preview of how every AI-adjacent failure will now be argued.

Why it matters
ExecutivesWrite down, today, how your organisation would demonstrate within hours that an AI system was not the cause of an outage. Air Space Intelligence had to do it on X on a Friday night.
SecurityThe question investigators are asking, whether an advisory system's data pipelines could load or destabilise an unrelated legacy path, is the same dependency question your integrations should already answer.
BeginnersNobody has shown the AI tool caused Friday's shutdown. Nobody in government has yet said it did not. Both things are true at once, and that is why this will run for weeks.

At some point on Friday afternoon the Federal Aviation Administration stopped every departing flight in the United States. The ground stop lasted roughly five hours, delayed or cancelled about 7,000 flights according to FlightAware figures cited by Al Jazeera, and was the first nationwide halt since a NOTAM database failure in January 2023; before that, the only comparable event was September 11, 2001. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy described a communication issue affecting all 22 Air Route Traffic Control Centers, and officials later attributed it to overloaded legacy telecom switches and routers that produced inconsistent data between facilities.

Why AI is in the story

Three days earlier, on November 10, the FAA had extended SMART, the Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories tool, from the Washington-area airports where it launched on September 21 to all 22 en-route centres. SMART is built by Air Space Intelligence under a 12-year contract worth about $875 million. It fuses roughly 200 live data streams, including airline schedules, weather, airport capacity and airspace constraints, and produces routing and scheduling recommendations that FAA specialists review and local managers decide whether to adopt. It is advisory. It does not control aircraft, and it reportedly runs on infrastructure separate from the STARS and ERAM systems involved in Friday's failure.

The proximity was enough. By Friday evening Air Space Intelligence posted that the stop was not based on or a result of SMART or its integration with the FAA. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, asked directly, said investigators were looking at it and that 'you can't rule out anything at this point'. Former DOT inspector general Mary Schiavo told CNN she did not know whether the two were linked but called the timing too much of a coincidence. No technical readout has been published.

The history that makes this harder

SMART arrived with a trust deficit. On October 13 more than two dozen House members, led by Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and others, asked the FAA to pause the tool until it explained how it had been tested in severe weather and equipment outages, what input controllers had, and how the evaluation period would be judged. Controllers told Congress they had little to no involvement in its design and feared it could produce hallucinated safety data. The FAA's position throughout has been that humans remain in charge. Congress approved $12.5 billion for air traffic modernisation last year and the administration has asked for a further $30.1 billion; the telecom gear that officials now blame is part of what that money is meant to replace.

The counter-argument

Everything public points to old hardware, not new software. The failure mode described, inconsistent data from overloaded switches, is the kind of fault the FAA has suffered repeatedly without any AI in the loop, including the New York and Philadelphia ground stops in September. An advisory tool that cannot issue clearances is an unlikely cause of a radio-communications failure. The most likely finding is that SMART was irrelevant to the outage and highly relevant to the politics of it.

What to watch

The FAA's post-incident report, and specifically whether it states that SMART's data feeds were examined and excluded; a bare 'telecom outage' finding will not end the argument. Whether Duffy pauses the nationwide rollout voluntarily, as the October letter requested. Whether the House members who signed the October letter get the hearing they asked for. And for anyone deploying AI into critical operations: the lesson is not that the tool failed, it is that the operator could not immediately prove it had not.
